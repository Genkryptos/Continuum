Production RAG:
a learning module.

Everything that decides whether a retrieval-augmented system survives contact with real users — chunking, embeddings, ANN indexes, fusion, reranking, context budgets, evaluation, and the failure modes nobody warns you about.

How to read this. Two kinds of claim appear below, and they are marked differently on purpose.

Boxes labelled measured are numbers from Continuum's own benchmark runs, with the file that produced them. Some of those numbers are negative results — ideas that sounded right and did not survive measurement. They are here because they are the most useful part.

01The pipeline, and where quality is actually won

RAG is not one system. It is a chain of eight or nine independent subsystems, each of which can silently destroy the work of the one before it. The single most common production mistake is optimising the subsystem you find most interesting rather than the one that is currently losing you the most.

The asymmetry that governs everything

Retrieval sets a ceiling. Everything after it is a funnel. If the answer-bearing chunk is not in the retrieved set, no reranker, no prompt, and no frontier model can recover it — the information is simply absent. But if it is retrieved, every later stage can still lose it: the reranker can bury it, the budget can truncate it, the prompt can bias against it, the model can ignore it.

This gives you the first diagnostic question, and you should be able to answer it with a number before you touch anything:

Ask first

Of your failures, how many retrieved the right context and still got it wrong? That single ratio splits your work into two disjoint programmes. Retrieval-bound failures and reasoning-bound failures have no fixes in common, and teams routinely spend months on the wrong one.

Measured — and it inverted the roadmap

On a 500-question LongMemEval-S run, Continuum classified all 118 failures by whether the answer-bearing session had been retrieved:

Failure classCountImplication
Retrieval-bound (answer session missing)2fixable with better retrieval
Right context retrieved, still wrong116not a retrieval problem at all

Recall was ~99%. Every hour spent on embeddings, indexes, or fusion would have moved ~2 questions. The residual was dominated by counting and aggregation — 58 of 118 failures were "how many", "how long", "total" — concentrated in multi-session and knowledge-update questions.

Source: findings/roadmap_v3.md, classified from results/ws4_full500/judged.json.

Note what this does not say. It does not say retrieval is unimportant; it says retrieval was already solved in that configuration, on that benchmark. §4 shows the same system losing 25 points of recall under different conditions. Both measurements are correct. The lesson is that "is retrieval my problem?" is not a question with a permanent answer — it is a measurement you re-take whenever the shape of your corpus changes.

02Ingestion & chunking

Chunking is where most RAG systems are quietly ruined, because its damage is invisible: a badly-chunked corpus produces plausible retrievals and confidently wrong answers, and nothing in your logs looks broken.

Parsing comes before chunking, and it is not solved

Before you can chunk you must extract text, and extraction from real documents is genuinely hard. The failure modes that matter:

  • Tables flattened into prose. A financial table becomes a run-on line of numbers with no column association. Every number becomes retrievable and none is interpretable. Extract tables structurally (as Markdown or HTML) or exclude them and handle them separately.
  • Multi-column PDFs read across columns. Line-based extractors interleave two unrelated sentences. Use a layout-aware parser and spot-check with your own eyes on twenty documents.
  • Headers, footers, page numbers, watermarks repeated on every page. These dominate lexical retrieval and add pure noise to embeddings. Strip them with a frequency filter: any line appearing on >50% of pages is furniture.
  • Scanned images with no text layer. Silently produce empty documents. Detect zero-length extractions and route them to OCR or a queue — never let them enter the index as empty.
  • Encoding and ligature damage — ﬁ as one glyph, smart quotes, soft hyphens splitting words across line breaks. These break exact lexical matching in ways cosine similarity hides from you.
Do this

Before building anything else, dump 20 random parsed documents to plain text and read them. Not the metrics — the actual text. Nearly every team that does this finds at least one systematic extraction bug, and finds it in under an hour. It is the highest-yield hour in the entire project.

Chunk size: the real trade-off

Chunk size is a tension between two failure modes, and the framing that matters is that they are not symmetric:

Chunks too smallChunks too large
Context is severed — pronouns lose referents, a number loses its unit, a clause loses its condition. Retrieval looks fine; answers are wrong. The embedding averages several topics into a vector near none of them. Retrieval quietly degrades and dilutes the prompt.
Recoverable downstream by fetching neighbours. Not recoverable — the damage is baked into the vector at index time.

That asymmetry is the practical guidance: err small and expand at read time. A small chunk that retrieves well can have its neighbours pulled in; a large chunk with a muddy embedding never surfaces at all.

Starting points

  • Prose / documentation: 300–500 tokens, 10–20% overlap.
  • Chat and conversational logs: chunk on turn or session boundaries, never on token count. A turn is the natural semantic unit and splitting it destroys speaker attribution.
  • Code: chunk on syntactic boundaries (function, class) using a real parser. Token-window chunking of code is close to useless — it splits function bodies from signatures.
  • Structured records: one row/record per chunk, serialised with its column names inline so the field names are embedded alongside the values.

Overlap, and why it is a weaker tool than it looks

Overlap exists to stop an answer being severed exactly at a boundary. It costs index size and creates near-duplicate retrievals that consume top-k slots. Two better tools exist and should be preferred:

  • Boundary-aware splitting — split at paragraph and section boundaries, never mid-sentence. This removes most of the need for overlap.
  • Neighbour expansion at read time — retrieve the chunk, then fetch chunk[i-1] and chunk[i+1] for the prompt. You get large-chunk context with small-chunk retrieval precision, and you pay the cost only on retrieved chunks rather than on the whole index.

Contextual enrichment

A chunk stripped of its document context is much harder to retrieve. Prepending a short breadcrumb — document title, section heading, date — measurably helps, because the query often matches the context rather than the chunk body.

[Employee Handbook 2026 > Section 4: Leave Policy > Parental leave]
Employees with 12 months' continuous service are entitled to...

Cheap, deterministic, and it makes the chunk retrievable by "what's the parental leave policy" even when the body never says "parental leave policy" as a phrase.

Watch for

If you enrich with an LLM (generating a summary per chunk), you have added a non-deterministic step to your index build. Re-indexing now produces a different corpus, and any A/B comparison across a re-index is confounded. Cache the enrichment keyed by content hash, or accept that your index is no longer reproducible.

Deduplication

Real corpora are full of near-duplicates: doc versions, boilerplate, quoted email threads. Duplicates consume your top-k with copies of one fact, starving the prompt of diversity. Dedupe at index time with a content hash for exact matches, and MinHash/SimHash or an embedding-similarity threshold for near-duplicates. Keep the newest and record what you dropped.

03Embeddings

The embedding model defines what "similar" means in your system. Every downstream component inherits that definition and none can override it.

What to actually check

PropertyWhy it decides things
DimensionalityDrives index size and memory linearly. 1024-dim at 4 bytes = 4 KB/vector; a million vectors is 4 GB before the index graph. Halving precision halves it (see §4).
Max sequence lengthText beyond it is silently truncated by most libraries. If your chunks exceed it, you are embedding prefixes and will never know.
Asymmetric vs symmetricMany models are trained with distinct query and document prefixes (query: / passage:). Omitting them costs real accuracy and is the single most common silent misuse.
NormalisationIf vectors are L2-normalised, cosine and inner product rank identically. If not, they differ. Know which you have; mismatches produce subtly wrong ordering.
Domain fitGeneral models underperform on code, legal, medical, and heavily-jargoned corpora. This is where a bake-off pays.
MultilingualIf your corpus or queries are multilingual, a monolingual model will fail in ways that look like retrieval bugs.
Measured — the embedder was not the bottleneck

Continuum ran a bake-off at 20k distractors on a realistic store. mxbai-embed-large found 19/20 needles; multilingual-e5-large found 16/20. Exact cosine over the stored vectors found 19/20 — meaning the embeddings contained the answers. What was shipping retrieved only 13/20.

The gap was not the model. It was the index (§4). "Switch embedding models" was ruled out with numbers rather than argued about.

Source: commit 0e31ae4, "the HNSW index, not the embedder, is what costs recall at scale".

Fine-tuning: usually not yet

Fine-tuning an embedding model is a real lever with a real cost: you must re-embed and re-index the entire corpus on every model change, and you now own an artefact that must be versioned alongside the index. Exhaust chunking, hybrid retrieval, and reranking first — they are cheaper and usually larger.

Do not

Never mix embeddings from different models, versions, or prefix conventions in one index. The vectors occupy incompatible spaces and similarity between them is meaningless — but nothing errors. Retrieval just degrades in a way no metric attributes correctly. Store the model id and version on every vector and refuse queries whose embedding model does not match the index.

04Vector storage & ANN indexes

This is the section most tutorials get wrong, usually by framing it as a vendor choice. It is not. It is a recall/latency trade-off, and the vendor mostly determines which knobs you get to turn.

Exact vs approximate

Exact nearest-neighbour search compares the query to every vector. It is perfectly accurate and linear in corpus size. Approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) indexes trade accuracy for speed by only visiting part of the space — and every ANN index has a recall below 1.0 that you must measure, because it is not printed on the box.

The three families

FamilyStructureCharacter
Flat / exactNo index; brute-force scanPerfect recall. Fine to ~10⁴–10⁵ vectors, and far more often the right answer than people expect.
IVFCluster into lists; search the nearest nprobe listsNeeds a training step on representative data. Recall depends on nprobe. Degrades if data distribution shifts after training.
HNSWMulti-layer proximity graph; m links per node, greedy descentStrong recall/latency, no training step, higher memory. The default in most modern stores.

HNSW parameters, precisely

  • m — bidirectional links per node. Higher = better recall, more memory, slower build. Typical 16–64.
  • ef_construction — beam width during build. Higher = better graph quality, slower build, no query cost. Typical 64–400.
  • ef_search — beam width at query time. The only one you can change without rebuilding. Higher = better recall, slower queries.

The critical property: m and ef_construction are baked into the graph at build time. If they are too low, no query-time setting fully recovers the lost recall — you must rebuild the index.

Measured — the index discarded a quarter of retrievable memories

Same 45,020-memory store, same embeddings, same query path. The only variable is whether the ANN index is used:

Configurationrecall@8p50 latency
With the HNSW index (what shipped)13/20 = 65%132 ms
Index dropped — exact scan18/20 = 90%157 ms

On that store, the index was trading 25 points of recall for 25 milliseconds. Raising ef_search could not rescue it — pgvector caps the setting at 1000, and it was already there.

Then the same experiment, run again on a different corpus, gave the opposite answer — and that is the actual lesson. A later run at 50,000 rows measured HNSW at 15% against exact search's 90%, an even wider gap. It looked like a devastating result. It was an artifact: the 50,000 distractors were generated near-duplicates, which collapse in embedding space into one dense cluster with the planted needles as isolated outliers — close to the worst possible case for a proximity graph. Re-run with distractors drawn from a real conversational dataset, changing nothing else, the gap vanished entirely: exact and HNSW both returned 85%, and HNSW was 20× faster.

Source: commit 0e31ae4. Continuum's later migration rebuilds at m=32, ef_construction=200.

The experiment everyone should run and almost nobody does

Drop your ANN index and measure recall with an exact scan on the same queries. The difference is what your index is costing you. It takes an afternoon, needs no new infrastructure, and is the only way to learn this number — no vendor publishes it for your data, because it depends on your data.

If the gap is large and your corpus is small enough to scan, dropping the index may genuinely be the right call. But run it on your own data — the number is a property of how your embeddings are distributed, not of the index. The same code and the same parameters gave −75pp on synthetic text and 0pp on real text.

Quantization and precision

Vectors can be stored at reduced precision to cut memory: half-precision floats (2 bytes/dim vs 4), scalar quantization to int8, or binary quantization to 1 bit/dim. Each trades accuracy for footprint. Half precision is typically near-lossless for retrieval; binary is aggressive and normally needs a rescoring pass over full-precision vectors for the top candidates.

Continuum stores halfvec(1024) — half precision, 2 KB per vector — with a cosine HNSW index.

Filtered search: the hard part nobody mentions

Real queries are almost never pure similarity. They are "similar and owned by this tenant and not deleted and valid as of this date". How your store combines filters with ANN determines whether that works:

  • Pre-filtering — restrict the candidate set, then search. Exact, but can be slow, and defeats the graph structure if the filter is very selective.
  • Post-filtering — search, then filter results. Fast, but can return fewer than k results, or none, when the filter is selective. This is a real production outage mode: a tenant with few documents gets empty results because everything in the global top-k belonged to someone else.
Test this explicitly

Write a test with a highly selective filter — a tenant owning 0.1% of the corpus — and assert you get k results back. Post-filtering systems fail this, and they fail it in production, for your smallest customers, who are also the ones least likely to file a good bug report.

Choosing a store

Given the above, the honest comparison is not about ANN quality — they all use the same family of algorithms — but about everything around it:

ConsiderationEmbedded in your OLTP database (e.g. pgvector)Dedicated vector service
Transactional consistencyVectors and business rows commit together. No skew possible.Two stores, two writes. A window exists where they disagree.
Filtered / joined queriesFull SQL: joins, temporal ranges, JSONB containment, all in one statement.Metadata filtering only, with the pre/post semantics above.
Operational surfaceOne system to back up, monitor, secure.A second system, with its own failure modes and bill.
Scale ceilingComfortable to ~10⁶–10⁷ vectors. Rebuilds get painful.Built for 10⁸+, sharding, high write churn.
The decisive question

Does a stale vector cause a correctness bug, or just a slightly worse result?

If your system must reflect that a fact was superseded — a policy changed, a customer moved, a price updated — then a separate vector store means a window in which the retrieval layer confidently returns something the database already knows is false. That is not a performance issue; it is a correctness issue, and it is architectural rather than fixable. Keep the vectors in the transaction.

If your corpus is static reference material, that concern evaporates and you should choose on scale and cost.

05Retrieval & hybrid fusion

Two channels with genuinely different strengths

Dense (vector) retrieval matches meaning. It finds "how do I reset my password" from "credential recovery procedure". It is weak on rare tokens: product SKUs, error codes, surnames, version numbers — because those carry little semantic content and get smoothed away.

Sparse (lexical) retrieval — BM25, or trigram similarity — matches tokens. It nails ERR_4021 and "Mahalanobis" exactly, and fails completely on paraphrase.

The standard conclusion is that you want both. That conclusion is right often enough to be the default — and it deserves to be checked rather than assumed.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion

To merge two ranked lists whose scores are not comparable — cosine similarity and BM25 live on different scales — RRF discards the scores and uses only ranks:

RRF(d) = Σ  1 / (k + rank_i(d))        # k ≈ 60 by convention
         i∈channels

Its virtues are that it needs no score normalisation, no tuning, and no training, and it rewards documents ranked decently by both channels over documents ranked first by one and absent from the other. Its cost is that it throws away score magnitude — a document that is overwhelmingly the best match is treated the same as one that merely edged out second place.

Over-fetch, or fusion does nothing

Each channel must return more candidates than you finally need. If both channels return exactly k, fusion has nothing to work with — a document ranked 11th by dense and 2nd by sparse never enters the pool. Fetch 3k to 5k per channel and fuse down.

Continuum's implementation runs both channels and the fusion in a single SQL statement, over-fetching to max(3k, 20) per channel, with tenant, soft-delete, and temporal-validity predicates pushed into both:

WITH dense AS (
  SELECT id, row_number() OVER (ORDER BY embedding <=> :q) AS rk
  FROM memory_nodes
  WHERE invalidated_at IS NULL AND namespace = :ns
  ORDER BY embedding <=> :q LIMIT :cand
), sparse AS (
  SELECT id, row_number() OVER (ORDER BY similarity(text, :t) DESC) AS rk
  FROM memory_nodes
  WHERE invalidated_at IS NULL AND namespace = :ns AND text %% :t
  ORDER BY similarity(text, :t) DESC LIMIT :cand
)
SELECT n.*, (1.0/(60 + d.rk) + 1.0/(60 + s.rk)) AS rrf
FROM memory_nodes n
LEFT JOIN dense d ON d.id = n.id
LEFT JOIN sparse s ON s.id = n.id
WHERE ...  ORDER BY rrf DESC  LIMIT :k
Measured — a null result, reported honestly

Continuum compared its shipped hybrid retriever against plain pgvector cosine on a 20-needle set at three corpus sizes (MRR):

CorpusHybridCosine only
3k.916.916
25k.900.903
47k.800.750

Every gap is roughly one needle out of twenty — within HNSW build noise. The honest claim is hybrid ≥ cosine, no clean win on this set, not "hybrid beats cosine".

The lesson is not that hybrid is useless. It is that a 20-needle evaluation set cannot resolve a 3-point difference, and reporting the null instead of the flattering subset is what makes the rest of the numbers trustworthy.

A later experiment closed this properly, and it is worth following the reasoning. The null above could not justify removing the lexical channel, because all 20 needles were paraphrase queries — precisely the regime where lexical matching is not supposed to help. Concluding "hybrid adds nothing" from them would have been the same error as testing a vector store with an exact-string needle. So the set was extended with 10 identifier needles, where the query shares a rare literal with the fact (ERR_4021, a version tag, a CVE id, a hyphenated surname) — the one regime with a mechanism argument for trigram matching.

Result on 50,000 rows of real text, same fixture, only the channel gated: identifier recall 100% either way, paraphrase recall 85% either way, and the miss sets byte-identical — the lexical channel returned zero documents the dense channel had not already found. It cost 2,276 ms per query. Modern subword tokenisation evidently preserves rare literals well enough that the classic argument for lexical retrieval does not hold for this embedder.

Note the shape of that argument, because it generalises: a null result was not treated as licence to remove the component. It was treated as a reason to go build the test that could actually settle it.

Source: commit 03715dd, scripts/retrieval_metrics.py.

Query transformation

  • Multi-query expansion — generate 3–5 paraphrases, retrieve for each, fuse. Improves recall on badly-phrased queries. Costs an LLM call and latency.
  • HyDE — have the model hallucinate an answer, embed that, and retrieve with it. Works because a hypothetical answer sits closer in embedding space to a real answer than a question does.
  • Decomposition — split multi-hop questions into sub-questions, retrieve per sub-question, then combine. Essential for "compare X and Y" queries, where a single embedding lands between the two topics and near neither.
  • Query routing — classify the query and pick a strategy. A cheap deterministic classifier is often enough and adds no latency.
Cost discipline

Every one of these adds an LLM call before retrieval — on the latency-critical path, on every query, including the majority that did not need it. Gate them behind a classifier so you pay only where they help, and measure the gated version, not the always-on version.

06Reranking

Retrieval embeds query and document independently — the document vector was computed before your query existed, so it cannot account for it. A cross-encoder reranker reads the query and document together and scores the pair jointly. It is far more accurate and far too slow to run over a corpus, which is exactly why it belongs in second position.

retrieve top 80 (cheap, recall-oriented)
   → rerank jointly        (expensive, precision-oriented)
      → keep top 24 for the prompt
The mistake that makes reranking a no-op

If your retriever returns 10 candidates and you rerank to 10, you have changed nothing but the order — and paid full price for it. Reranking only creates value when the candidate pool is substantially larger than what you keep. Over-fetch aggressively: retrieve 5–10× your keep count.

Where reranking hurts

Rerankers optimise topical relevance to the query. When the task needs something else, that objective is actively wrong:

  • Aggregation — "how many projects have I mentioned?" needs every instance, not the most relevant one. Reranking to a tight top-k is precisely the wrong move.
  • Preference and subjective questions — the turns that establish a preference are often not the most topically similar to the question.
  • Temporal ordering — relevance ranking scrambles chronology, which is fatal for anything asking what happened first.
Measured — two order-of-presentation findings

Reranking is gated off for preference questions in Continuum's pipeline: it measurably helped the retrieval-bound categories (knowledge-update, multi-session) and hurt preference, by reordering away the turns the preference rubric needs.

Chronological presentation was worth ~25pp on event-ordering questions. Select the top-k by relevance as usual — then present them in chronological order rather than score order. Cosine top-k otherwise hands the model a scrambled timeline. The retrieved set is identical; only the ordering changed.

Source: --chrono-sort in evals/longmemeval/bootstrap_ollama.py; BEAM evaluation findings.

That second result generalises further than it looks. The order in which you present retrieved context is a free variable that most systems never tune, and models are demonstrably sensitive to position. Relevance order is a default, not a law.

07Context assembly & token budgets

You now have your final chunks. What you do in the next twenty lines of code decides more than most of the preceding pipeline, and it is almost always the least-reviewed code in the system.

Enforce budgets on whole items, never on characters

The natural implementation is a string slice:

# WRONG — cuts mid-sentence, mid-word, mid-token
context = "\n".join(chunks)[:max_chars]

This truncates the final chunk mid-sentence. At a generous budget it is cosmetic. At a tight budget it hands the model a fragment that ends mid-clause — and the model does what a careful reader would: treats the mangled tail as unreliable.

# RIGHT — admit whole items, stop at the first that would overflow
admitted, used = [], 0
for chunk in chunks:
    cost = count_tokens(chunk) + (1 if admitted else 0)
    if used + cost > budget:
        break
    admitted.append(chunk); used += cost
context = "\n".join(admitted)

Stopping at the first overflow — rather than skipping and continuing — keeps the budget monotone: raising it only ever adds items, never swaps one for another. Without that property, two budget settings are not comparable, and any ablation across them is measuring noise.

Count tokens, do not estimate them

Measured — the assumed ratio was wrong by 18%

"~4 characters per token" is the universal rule of thumb. Measured with a real tokenizer over actual conversational turns, Continuum's corpus came out at 4.70 chars/token — and 4.1 on synthetic text, and 3.9 on a single hand-picked line.

At a 64,000-character budget that is the difference between believing you are sending 16,000 tokens and actually sending 13,600. Every cost projection built on the estimate was wrong by nearly a fifth, in the direction of overestimating what you could fit.

Source: evals/longmemeval/budget.py; ratio now reported per run rather than assumed.

Verify the budget actually binds

Measured — the budget flag was doing nothing at all

Continuum's flagship configuration ran with --max-context-chars 64000. Instrumenting the assembled prompt revealed the retriever's actual output: p50 26,574 chars, maximum 44,346. The 64,000 cap never bound on a single row.

The real ceiling was --rerank-to 24 — every row admitted exactly 24 items. A planned five-point budget ablation would have spent its top point measuring a no-op and its second on a cap that clipped only 30% of rows.

Found by a pct_rows_budget_bound metric added specifically to catch this class of error, on its first run.

Instrument this permanently

Log, on every request: tokens actually sent, items admitted vs offered, and whether the budget bound. Without the third, a config change that does nothing is indistinguishable from one that works, and you will attribute unrelated noise to it.

Prompt structure

  • Delimit context unambiguously so the model can tell instructions from retrieved data. This is a correctness measure and a security measure (§13).
  • Attribute every chunk with a stable id, so the model can cite and you can verify.
  • Put the question after the context for long contexts — recency helps.
  • Give an explicit abstention instruction. Without one, models answer from parametric memory when retrieval comes back empty, producing exactly the confident hallucinations RAG was adopted to prevent.
  • Order deliberately — chronological for temporal questions, relevance otherwise (§6).

08Generation

Grounding and citation

Require the model to cite chunk ids inline, then verify the citations in code: does the cited id exist in what you sent? A model that cites a chunk it was not given is a detectable failure — and one of the very few hallucination classes you can catch deterministically, without a judge.

Abstention is a feature

"I don't have that information" is the correct answer when retrieval fails, and systems are routinely tuned to suppress it because it feels like failure. Track your abstention rate in both directions: abstaining when the answer was present is a retrieval or prompt problem; answering when it was absent is a hallucination problem. Both are bugs; they have opposite fixes.

What does not work

Measured — four negative results worth inheriting

Continuum built and measured four post-retrieval mechanisms. All four were net-negative or within noise:

  • Computing counts in code from extracted structure
  • A deterministic answer router bypassing the reader
  • Evidence distillation before answering
  • Temporal date arithmetic in code

The stated reason is worth generalising: you cannot bolt deterministic machinery onto a mid-size model's unreliable intermediate outputs. Each mechanism assumed a structured extraction step was accurate enough to compute on. It wasn't, and the deterministic layer amplified the errors instead of absorbing them.

Source: docs/report.md, "What we tried that didn't work".

Model choice, and the ceiling question

Accuracy has two ceilings: what the memory layer can supply, and what the reader can do with it. If you cannot tell which one binds, run a single evaluation with a much stronger reader on an unchanged pipeline. If accuracy jumps, you were reader-bound and retrieval work was misdirected. If it does not, the ceiling is architectural and a bigger model will not save you.

09Freshness, updates & state

Most RAG tutorials assume a static corpus. Production corpora change, and change is where naive RAG breaks worst — because the old version of a fact is still perfectly retrievable, and often more so.

The superseded-fact problem

A user says "I live in Boston." Later: "I moved to NYC." Both are indexed. Both are semantically similar to "where do I live?" — and the older one may rank higher, having accumulated more corroborating context. The system confidently returns a fact it already knows to be obsolete.

Chunk-level timestamps do not fix this. The model sees two plausible statements and no reliable signal about which supersedes which; "most recent wins" fails as soon as someone mentions a past state in the present tense.

What actually addresses it

  1. Extract atomic facts rather than storing only raw text — subject, attribute, value.
  2. Detect contradiction on write. When a new fact contradicts an existing one about the same subject/attribute, mark the old one superseded rather than deleting it.
  3. Filter to current state at query time by default, so superseded facts cannot compete for top-k.
  4. Keep bi-temporal validity — both when a fact was true in the world and when the system learned it — so "what was true as of March?" is answerable and auditable.

This is Continuum's central thesis: memory for agents is a state problem, not a retrieval problem. Its deterministic supersession and as-of-date benchmarks are the numbers it reports at 100%, precisely because they do not depend on a model's judgement.

The architectural consequence

If superseded facts must be excluded at query time, that exclusion has to be atomic with the write that superseded them. Split your vectors into a separate store from your state and you have created a window where retrieval returns what the database already knows is false. For a static reference corpus this does not matter. For anything modelling a changing world, it is the whole ballgame.

Re-indexing

Treat the index as a build artefact: versioned, reproducible, rebuildable. Prefer incremental updates keyed by content hash so unchanged documents are not re-embedded. For full rebuilds — a new embedding model, new chunking — build into a new index and swap atomically. Never mutate a live index in place; you will serve a half-reindexed corpus and the symptom will be inexplicable.

10Evaluation — the part that decides everything

Everything above is guesswork without this section. A RAG system you cannot measure is a RAG system you cannot improve, and most of the field's folklore exists because someone shipped a change that felt better.

Measure the stages separately

End-to-end accuracy alone cannot tell you which stage failed. Instrument each:

StageMetricWhat it answers
RetrievalRecall@kWas the answer-bearing chunk retrieved at all? The ceiling on everything downstream.
RankingMRR, NDCG@kHow high did it rank? MRR for one right answer; NDCG when relevance is graded.
RerankRecall@k post-rerankDid the reranker drop the gold chunk? Accuracy alone conflates this with reader error.
AssemblyContext tokens, items dropped, budget bound?Did the gold chunk survive truncation?
GenerationJudged accuracy, abstention rate, citation validityGiven correct context, did it answer correctly?

Scoring: substring matching will mislead you

Measured — a scorer artifact that reads as a total failure

Substring matching undercounts by roughly 9 percentage points uniformly, because a correctly paraphrased answer scores as wrong.

Worse, it is not uniform across categories. On single-session-preference — questions asking the user to choose between named options — substring scoring reports 0.0%. LLM-judged accuracy on the same rows is ~57%. A team reading the substring number would conclude the category was completely broken and rebuild a component that worked.

Source: findings/longmemeval_2026-05.md.

Use an LLM judge for the primary number, keep substring as a cheap reference column, and pin the judge model — changing judges silently rebases every historical result.

Always break out by category

Aggregate accuracy hides everything actionable. The same system, same run:

Variance, and the trap of the flattering run

This invalidates more experiments than anything else

Continuum's reader shows ±3–5pp run-to-run variance on an unchanged configuration — the same code, the same data, a different sample. The measured headline is ~74% with a 73.6–75.6% observed range.

Any improvement smaller than that range cannot be distinguished from noise by a single run. Continuum's own documentation states that its read-side additions are within noise of the control, and that the reproducible wins are the deterministic benches. Reporting that costs a headline number and buys the credibility of every other number.

Practically: run your control more than once, know your variance before you believe any delta, and report confidence intervals. Wilson intervals are appropriate for accuracy proportions.

Build the evaluation set before the system

  • 100–200 questions minimum for aggregate signal; more if you need per-category resolution. A 20-item set cannot resolve a 3-point difference (§5).
  • Stratify by category in proportion to real traffic, so the aggregate is comparable to production.
  • Record gold chunk ids, not just gold answers — that is what makes Recall@k computable and stage attribution possible.
  • Include abstention cases — questions whose answers are genuinely absent. Without them you cannot measure hallucination at all.
  • Freeze it. Every configuration must run on byte-identical rows or comparisons are meaningless.
Ablate one variable at a time

Change one thing, hold everything else fixed, re-run the frozen set. It is slow and it is the only method that produces knowledge rather than folklore. When Continuum ran a budget ablation, only --max-context-chars moved across five runs — which is what made the resulting curve interpretable, and what surfaced the fact that the flag had never bound.

11Failure taxonomy & debugging

When a query returns a wrong answer, work the pipeline in order and stop at the first stage that fails. Guessing wastes weeks.

  1. Is the document in the corpus at all? Query by id. Parsing and ingestion failures are common and invisible — empty extractions from scanned PDFs are the classic.
  2. Is the answer intact within one chunk? Print the chunk. If a boundary split the answer, it is a chunking bug and nothing downstream can fix it.
  3. Does exact search find it? Brute-force scan the embeddings. If exact finds it and your ANN index does not, the index is your problem (§4) — and you now have the number.
  4. Did retrieval return it? Compute Recall@k. If no, compare dense and sparse channels separately: dense-only misses often mean rare tokens; sparse-only misses mean paraphrase.
  5. Did the reranker keep it? Compare Recall@k before and after. Rerankers drop gold chunks, especially for aggregation and preference questions.
  6. Did it survive assembly? Check items dropped and whether the budget bound. This is where the byte-slice bug (§7) lives.
  7. Was it in the final prompt? Log the assembled prompt. Surprisingly often, it is not.
  8. Then, and only then, is it a reasoning failure. Classify further: aggregation, temporal arithmetic, inference, or an abstention judgement.
Make this cheap

Build a trace that dumps every stage's input and output for one query id. The stage-by-stage walk above should take minutes, not a day of instrumenting. If it currently takes a day, that tooling is your highest-value next task — higher than any model or index change.

12Cost & latency engineering

Know your unit economics precisely

A real failure worth copying the fix for

Continuum's telemetry priced two models and returned 0.0 for anything else. The reader in every headline run was not in that table — so every benchmark run reported a cost of approximately zero, indistinguishable from a free model, for months.

The fix is one line of policy: a model with no pricing entry must raise a warning, not silently cost nothing. Silence and free are different states and must never render identically.

Where the money goes

  • Input tokens dominate. RAG prompts are long and answers are short — often 20–50× more input than output.
  • Retrieved context is the largest single line item, which is exactly why budget ablation is the highest-leverage cost work available.
  • Pre-retrieval LLM calls (query expansion, HyDE, routing) are paid on every query, including the ones that did not need them.
  • Embedding at index time is a large one-off, repeated in full on every model change.

Latency budget

A rough decomposition worth measuring on your own stack: query embedding 10–50 ms · ANN search 5–150 ms (§4 — this varies enormously with ef_search) · reranking 50–200 ms · generation 1–10 s. Generation dominates. Optimising a 20 ms retrieval path underneath a 4-second generation is a rounding error, and it is a very common way to spend a sprint.

Caching, in increasing order of yield

  • Embedding cache keyed by content hash — trivially correct, large savings on re-index.
  • Retrieval cache keyed by normalised query + filters — must be invalidated on write, which is the hard part.
  • Prompt-prefix caching — most providers charge substantially less for a repeated prefix. Put stable content first and volatile content last, or you forfeit it entirely.
  • Full response cache for exact repeats — narrow, but free.

Routing by budget class

Not every query needs the maximum context. If simple lookups are answerable from a handful of facts while multi-hop questions need wide context, a cheap deterministic classifier can assign a budget per query and cut average tokens substantially at near-constant accuracy.

Two guards this needs

Measure the mis-route rate and accuracy on mis-routed queries. A router that is 95% accurate but craters the 5% it gets wrong is worse than no router, and aggregate accuracy will hide that.

Fail open. If the model abstains under a small budget, retry once at the large budget and count the retry in your cost. That converts a routing error from a wrong answer into a slightly higher bill — the right direction to fail.

13Security & privacy

Indirect prompt injection

Your retrieved chunks come from documents. If an attacker can influence any document you index — a support ticket, a public wiki page, a shared file, a web page — they can place instructions in your model's context. This is not theoretical; it is the defining vulnerability class of RAG.

  • Delimit and label retrieved content explicitly as untrusted data, and instruct the model that content inside those delimiters is never to be followed as instructions.
  • Never let retrieved content trigger tool calls without an independent authorisation check. Retrieval-to-action is the path from injection to real damage.
  • Treat every document with the trust level of whoever can write to it.
  • Log what entered each prompt, so an incident is reconstructable.

Access control

The breach pattern

Filtering results after retrieval is not access control. If you retrieve globally and then remove what the user may not see, the document's content has already influenced ranking, and any scoring, count, or "no results" signal you expose leaks its existence.

Enforce tenancy inside the retrieval query — as a predicate the index applies, not a filter you apply to its output. Then test it with a tenant owning a tiny fraction of the corpus (§4).

PII and deletion

Right-to-deletion applies to your vector index too. Deleting a source row while its embedding remains retrievable is a compliance failure with a clear audit trail. Make deletion cascade to vectors, derived chunks, and caches — and test it, because ANN indexes frequently implement deletion as a tombstone that still occupies the graph.

Note also that embeddings are not anonymisation. Inversion attacks can recover substantial source text from vectors; treat an embedding as containing its source content.

14Operations & observability

Log per request

  • Query, normalised query, and any transformation applied
  • Retrieved ids with per-channel scores and ranks
  • Post-rerank ids and scores
  • Context tokens sent, items admitted vs offered, whether the budget bound
  • Model, prompt-token and completion-token counts, cost
  • Per-stage latency
  • Abstention flag and citation-validity result

Alert on

  • Abstention rate moving in either direction — the earliest signal of a retrieval regression, and it needs no labels
  • Empty-result rate, especially per tenant
  • p95 latency per stage, not just end-to-end
  • Cost per query drifting
  • Index size and freshness lag
Run the frozen set in CI

A nightly evaluation on your frozen set, with per-category breakout and the run-to-run variance band drawn on the chart, turns silent regressions into visible ones. Given the variance in §10, a single nightly number will produce false alarms — plot the band, not the point.

15Scaling

CorpusWhat is appropriate
< 10k chunksExact search. No ANN index. Perfect recall, adequate latency, one fewer thing to get wrong. Most projects are here and index prematurely.
10k – 1MHNSW in your existing database. Measure the recall cost against exact (§4) and tune m / ef_construction deliberately.
1M – 10MTuned HNSW, quantization, careful filtered-search design. Rebuild time becomes an operational constraint you must plan around.
> 10MDedicated vector infrastructure, sharding, distributed index builds. Now the vendor question is real.

Two scaling effects are worth internalising because they are counter-intuitive:

  • Recall degrades with corpus size at fixed index parameters. Settings tuned at 10k will underperform at 100k. Re-measure at each order of magnitude rather than assuming.
  • More documents means more near-misses, not just more candidates. Precision problems that were invisible at small scale become dominant, and reranking matters more the larger you get.

17The launch checklist

Before you build

  • Frozen evaluation set, 100+ questions, stratified, with gold chunk ids and abstention cases
  • Control run repeated 3× so you know your variance band
  • Per-stage metrics wired: Recall@k, MRR, judged accuracy, context tokens, cost

Retrieval

  • 20 parsed documents read by a human
  • Chunking validated — the answer is intact within a chunk
  • Embedding model, version, and prefix convention pinned and stored per vector
  • ANN recall measured against exact search
  • Over-fetch confirmed larger than rerank keep count
  • Selective-filter test passes (tenant with 0.1% of corpus returns k results)

Assembly & generation

  • Budget enforced on whole items; no mid-item truncation
  • Tokens counted with a real tokenizer; chars/token measured and reported
  • Budget confirmed to actually bind
  • Context delimited and labelled untrusted; abstention instruction present
  • Citations verified in code against what was sent
  • Presentation order chosen deliberately (chronological where it matters)

Production

  • Every model priced; unpriced models raise a warning rather than costing zero
  • Tenancy enforced inside the query, not after it
  • Deletion cascades to vectors and caches, and is tested
  • Per-request logging complete; abstention-rate alert live
  • Nightly evaluation in CI with the variance band plotted
  • Index rebuildable and versioned; swaps are atomic
If you take one thing

Every number in the boxes above exists because someone ran the experiment instead of reasoning about it — and several of them contradicted a confident, sensible prior. The embedder that turned out not to be the bottleneck. The hybrid retrieval that produced a null. The budget flag that had never once bound. The four elegant deterministic layers that all made things worse.

Production RAG is not a stack you assemble. It is a measurement loop you run — and the value comes disproportionately from the measurements that tell you that you were wrong.
